About

Youngjun Lee, Ph.D., is a Postdoctoral Research Fellow in the Division of Gastroenterology, Hepatology and Nutrition at Children's Hospital of Philadelphia and an affiliated postdoctoral researcher in the Annenberg Extended Reality Lab at the University of Pennsylvania. His research aims to make physical activity work for the mental health of autistic people. Using national surveys, cohorts, health records, and wearable data, he studies who benefits from physical activity and why autistic people may benefit less. He also develops immersive VR and XR exercise, working toward sessions that are personalized in real time with heart rate and heart rate variability and guided by an AI virtual coach. He earned his Ph.D. in Kinesiology from Michigan State University, where his dissertation tested a virtual reality boxing program for autistic young adults, and his master's and bachelor's degrees in physical education from Korea University.

Research

My research starts from one question: how can physical activity solve mental health problems? I first explored it in general populations, using national surveys in Korea and the United States to link physical activity, diet, and sleep with metabolic and mental health.

During my Ph.D. at Michigan State University, I turned to people with disabilities, and especially to autistic people. They are often excluded from traditional exercise and sport, so they miss out on its benefits. I first saw this as a problem of barriers. But the more I studied, the more it appeared that even when autistic people take part in traditional activity and sport, they may not gain the same mental health benefit as their non-autistic peers.

That calls for a different approach. I build immersive VR and XR exercise programs for autistic people. Because surveys capture their activity poorly, I measure activity, sleep, and daily rhythms with wearable devices. And I am working toward exercise that reads heart rate and heart rate variability in real time to personalize each session, with an AI-driven virtual coach that keeps it motivating and enjoyable, so that its mental health benefit is as large as possible.

My ultimate goal

To make physical activity an accessible, personalized, and evidence-based path to better mental health for every autistic person.

Who benefits from physical activity

Population, cohort, and clinical epidemiology

I use national surveys, birth cohorts, and pediatric health records to test when physical activity and sport protect against depression, anxiety, and suicidal behavior, and whether autistic people gain the same benefit as their non-autistic peers. My recent work suggests that for autistic children, simply being active on more days is not enough.

General populations

KNHANES, NHANES, HBSC, and KYRBS, linking activity, diet, and sleep to metabolic and mental health in Korea, the U.S., and 45 countries.

Autism and disability

NSCH, the UK Millennium Cohort Study, and CHOP pediatric health records, comparing autistic and non-autistic youth.

Form and context

The type, dose, and structure of activity, including organized sport, and how adversity changes its effects.

Measuring activity with wearables

Device-measured activity, sleep, and daily rhythms

Surveys and parent reports capture autistic people's activity poorly. I use accelerometer and Fitbit data to measure how much autistic people move and sleep and how regular their daily rhythms are, and test how these patterns relate to depression. At CHOP, this includes Fitbit data from the NIH All of Us Research Program.

Device-measured behavior

Activity, sleep, and rest-activity rhythms from Fitbit, GENEActiv, and ActiGraph devices.

Large wearable datasets

Fitbit records linked to health data in the NIH All of Us Research Program, comparing adults with and without autism.

Data quality

How missing wear time distorts estimates of activity and its link to depression, studied with simulation.

Selected work

  • Wearable-derived behavioral data for depression-related/internalizing outcomes monitoring in autistic individuals: A scoping review. Review Journal of Autism and Developmental Disorders, in revision.
  • Wearable-derived sleep and physical activity levels and rhythms and depressive symptoms in adults with and without autism spectrum disorder. SLEEP 2026, Baltimore, and Lurie Autism Institute Annual Symposium, 2026.

Adaptive XR exercise with an AI virtual coach

Immersive exercise, physiology, and personalization

I design immersive VR exergames that lower the sensory and social demands of gyms and team sports. My pilot randomized trials of VR boxing tested effects on depressive symptoms and heart rate variability, and lower resting heart rate variability predicted persistent simulator sickness. Next, I aim to use heart rate and heart rate variability from wearables in real time to adjust each session, and to add an AI-driven virtual coach that responds to each person, so that exercise is more motivating and enjoyable and its mental health benefit larger.

Immersive exercise

VR boxing and other exergames built around autistic users' sensory and social needs.

Real-time physiology

Heart rate and heart rate variability to tailor intensity and exposure to each person.

AI virtual coaching

An interactive coach that builds social presence, motivation, and enjoyment.

Data and methods

Datasets
National Survey of Children's Health (NSCH), NHANES, Health Behaviour in School-aged Children (HBSC), Korea Youth Risk Behavior Survey (KYRBS), KNHANES, UK Millennium Cohort Study, Korean national panel studies, NIH All of Us Research Program, pediatric electronic health records
Devices
Fitbit, GENEActiv, and ActiGraph wearables; Meta Quest 3 headsets
Measures
Physical activity, sport participation, sleep, and rest-activity rhythms; depression, anxiety, and suicidal behavior; heart rate and heart rate variability (RMSSD, SDNN, LF/HF); blood biomarkers
Study designs
Pilot randomized controlled trials, longitudinal cohort and panel analyses, multiverse and latent class analyses, simulation studies, mixed-methods process evaluations, systematic and scoping reviews following PRISMA
Open science
Preregistered secondary data analyses and reviews on OSF
Tools
R, SPSS, Python
